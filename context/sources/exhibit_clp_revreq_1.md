## II. SUMMARY OF REVENUE REQUIREMENT ANALYSIS

_Testimony page 6_

**Q.** Why does CL&P need to increase base rates?

**A.** The Company’s filing at this time is necessitated by the cost of significant investments in electric infrastructure made to deliver safe, reliable and resilient service to our customers. In total, the Company has installed approximately $3.3 billion in net utility plant since 2017 for the benefit of our customers, of which approximately $600 million is not currently included in rates. In addition to enabling a high level of service reliability to residential and business customers, the Company’s investments in electric distribution infrastructure generate approximately $240 million annually in state and local tax revenue for the 157 municipalities that Eversource serves in Connecticut. The remaining revenue deficiency that is not associated with infrastructure investments arises from core operating needs, including increases in operating and maintenance (“O&M”) expense and vegetation-management costs, along with increased costs to restore the electric system after typical inclement weather conditions and to fund restoration and response efforts necessitated by larger, major weather events that arise more sporadically. As a public service company, CL&P must have rates in place that allow it to meet its public service obligation to provide safe, reliable, and cost-effective service to our customers, including the recovery of its operating costs and the cost of capital to debt lenders and equity investors. Consistent with this obligation, the Company must continuously invest in its distribution system to ensure safe and reliable operation. As outlined in the testimony of Lavelle A. Freeman, David L. Gladey, Jennifer A. Schilling, Dr. Elli Ntakou and John DiPaola-Tromba, in Exhibit CLP-TSP-1, our electric infrastructure is classified as “essential critical infrastructure” that supports critical sectors (including healthcare, financial systems, communications and transportation and other public support systems), public health and safety systems, economic growth and security, while supporting many critical aspects of daily life for Connecticut residents. The Company takes our obligation very seriously and works each and every day to meet the challenges of that role in a responsible, prudent and diligent manner. As a result, although CL&P has made substantial capital investments in its system since its last rate case, it is critical to recognize that the need for on-going electric infrastructure investments will continue for the foreseeable future. Electric distribution facilities require constant attention in order to maintain a safe and reliable system for our customers, manage aging infrastructure, electrification trends and grid modernization efforts. These investments to maintain and improve the system’s safety, reliability and resiliency are a critical factor driving today’s need for a revenue increase. CL&P must increase its distribution rates to account for the system investment not currently reflected in rates and is proposing to implement a PBMRP that will provide revenue support for the significant distribution investment needs, focusing on affordability and providing rate stability through gradual annual rate increases, while also ensuring that Company incentives maintain alignment with that of our customers.

**Q.** Please describe the Company’s other rate-related proposals included in this proceeding and explain whether these proposals affect the computation of the revenue requirement.

**A.** In this proceeding, the Company is proposing to implement or continue various raterelated proposals, including:

- The discontinuation of the Company’s ESI. Section 5 of Public Act No. 23102 states, in part that “[i]n any proceeding to amend rates under section 16-19 of the general statutes… the Public Utilities Regulatory Authority shall not reauthorize the on-bill reconciling mechanism for new electric plant additions that was first authorized in 2018.” The Company proposes that costs and revenues associated with the capital previously recovered through the ESI be reflected in base distribution rates, with rates effective as a result of this proceeding. If implemented as proposed, this would discontinue the ESI mechanism in the future, although amounts for both costs and revenues prior to July 1, 2027 (the proposed rate effective date in this proceeding) will logistically require the reconciliation to continue for a period of time after rates are effective in this proceeding to conduct a final reconciliation of costs and revenues, which will not be known until after the rate effective date in this proceeding. This is further discussed in Section XII below in our testimony.

- The inclusion in base distribution rates of all revenue requirements associated with capital that is currently recovered through the Company’s Non-Bypassable Federally Mandated Congestion Charge (“NBFMCC”) and System Benefits Charge (“SBC”) reconciliation mechanisms. Note that, similar to the discussion above regarding the sunsetting of the ESI mechanism, the Company is proposing to reflect the costs of the capital currently recovered through the Company’s NBFMCC and SBC mechanisms in base rates, such that the current recovery will cease to operate in its current form after the implementation of new rates in this proceeding. However, due to the timing of the NBFMCC and SBC mechanisms and the fact that base rates in this proceeding will be set prospectively for effect July 1, 2027, and the fact that the NBFMCC and SBC mechanisms recover costs for capital additions not reflected in base rates, the mechanism will need to remain in place to allow for cost recovery of the costs incurred prior to July 1, 2027, but not yet recovered in base distribution rates as of July 1, 2027, and to reconcile any overor under-recovered balances for that time period, as well. Similarly, to the extent there is additional capital investment required to support the programs contained in the NBFMCC and SBC that is required after base rates are established in this proceeding, the Company will propose recovery of such costs in a future RAM proceeding for PURA’s review and approval. This proposal is discussed further below in our testimony.

- The continuation of a revenue decoupling mechanism consistent with Conn. Gen. Stat. §16-19-tt(b) with one modification to allow for carrying charges on any over/under-recovery consistent with all other Rate Adjustment Mechanism (“RAM”) components. This proposal is described in the testimony of the Rates Panel in Exhibit CLP-RATES-1 (sponsored by Company witnesses Edward A. Davis, Kimberly Parsons). The revenue decoupling mechanism allows for the reconciliation of the actual level of annual base distribution revenues against the approved distribution revenue requirement for the Rate Year resulting from this proceeding. The mechanics of the mechanism rely on, but do not affect, the revenue requirement in this proceeding; and

- As a result of a settlement agreement in Docket No. 17-10-46, Application of The Connecticut Light and Power Company d/b/a Eversource Energy to Amend its Rate Schedules, PURA approved an earning sharing mechanism that set an ROE threshold of 9.25 percent, with any earnings in excess of 9.25 percent shared automatically on a 50%-50% basis with customers. In this proceeding the Company proposes to continue this construct until the Company’s next base rate proceeding, such that the Company would automatically begin sharing any earnings in excess of the authorized ROE in this case on a 50%-50% basis with customers for any earnings in excess of the allowed ROE resulting from this proceeding.

**Q.** Why is the Company proposing rate base treatment for deferred assets and liabilities in this proceeding?

**A.** Deferred assets and liabilities reflect amounts that are deferred for future recovery from or refund to customers, generally at the time of a base rate proceeding, in the absence of a separate mechanism operating outside of base rates. PURA has historically provided for rate base treatment of regulatory assets and liabilities, but has not historically allowed carrying charges to accrue on deferred assets or liabilities prior to their incorporation into rate base as part of a rate case, except in certain circumstances. As a ratemaking practice, allowing the recovery of deferred assets and liabilities as a component of rate base has provided a level of compensatory recovery for the actual carrying charges incurred prior to a prudence determination, as the allowed return on rate base is determined at a single point in time and set in base rates at the time of a distribution rate case. It is important to highlight that this treatment is symmetrical to both regulatory assets and liabilities alike.

**Q.** Has this process been changed in recent distribution rate proceedings by PURA?

**A.** Yes. In Docket No. 22-08-08 (United Illuminating’s (“UI”) 2023 base distribution rate case) and other subsequent base distribution rate cases, PURA has adjusted this longstanding ratemaking practice and instead allowed for the recovery of all regulatory assets and liabilities over an identified period of time, outside of rate base, with carrying costs accruing at the pre-tax weighted average cost of capital (“WACC”) on a prospective basis over the recovery period. Additionally, PURA determined that (1) carrying charges on regulatory assets and liabilities are permissible only with express approval from PURA, (2) are only allowed prospectively during the recovery period and (3) allowing recovery of carrying charges on deferred costs that have not undergone a prudence determination contradicts general ratemaking principles.

**Q.** Please describe the flaws in rationale for these changes in Docket No. 22-08-08 and subsequent base rate proceedings.

**A.** The conclusions reached in Docket No. 22-08-08 are supported by a reference to the generally accepted accounting principles (“GAAP”) and more specifically Accounting Standards Codification (“ASC”) 980. The rationale to evaluate GAAP and ASC 980 was a result of a Connecticut Supreme Court decision which made reference to the Financial Accounting Standards Board (“FASB”) Statement No. 71, which is now codified under ASC 980. In the final decision, PURA first concluded that a regulatory asset exists at the point in which it is probable that a previously incurred cost will be recoverable in rates. The Company agrees this is an accurate representation of the accounting requirements under ASC 980 for any Company to recognize a regulatory asset under GAAP accounting rules. However, PURA goes on to conclude that allowing carrying costs prior to a prudence determination is not permissible because carrying costs do not meet the definition of an “incurred cost”, which is a requirement under the recognition criteria within ASC 980-340-25-1. This conclusion is factually incorrect. In the decision PURA references the distinction under GAAP between an “incurred cost” and an “allowable cost.” Under ASC 980-10-20 an incurred cost is defined as “a cost arising from cash paid out or obligation to pay for an acquired asset or service, a loss from any cause that has been sustained and must be paid for.” According to PURA’s decision carrying costs are not assets, services or losses that must be paid for and therefore are not an “incurred cost” chargeable to expense. This assertion ignores the fact that under GAAP and ASC 980, a debt-based carrying cost, which is a component of the weighted average cost of capital (“WACC”) does represent an incurred cost. The regulatory lag created by deferring recovery of prudently incurred costs requires the Company to increase its borrowing, which is financed through a combination of long-term debt and equity. The interest expense associated with that funding is a “service” and that service is “paid for” by the utility. Therefore, carrying charges plainly meet the definition of an incurred cost under ASC 980. PURA further concluded that carrying costs should not be applied at the time of cost incurrence because that would be inconsistent with the principle that rates are set on a prospective basis. PURA’s conclusions however are refuted by its own precedent where in the annual Rate Adjustment Mechanism (“RAM”) process under and over recoveries are allowed continuous carrying charges prior to a prudence determination. For any regulatory deferral, the utility is funding customer obligations with available capital resources, all of which come at a cost, which must be accounted for under GAAP and should be recognized under ratemaking principles. Likewise, customer funds collected by the utility and subject to refund should be treated equally for both accounting and ratemaking purposes. Finally, PURA determined in Docket No. 22-08-08 that allowing the recognition of carrying charges on deferred costs, would provide “extraordinary relief upon extraordinary relief” 6, noting that a regulatory asset is an extraordinary form of relief for utilities as it provides recovery of expenses beyond the amounts provided in base distribution rates. However, a regulatory deferral is a very common ratemaking practice that PURA has consistently adopted either through annual reconciliation mechanisms, such as the RAM mechanisms, or other base distribution rate deferrals such as Environmental Remediation costs or other amounts outside the utilities control such as Pole Make-Ready costs, both of which are outlined below. PURA determined in Docket No. 22-08-08 that permitting the recognition of carrying charges on deferred expenses prior to a prudence determination could incentivize utilities to delay a request for cost recovery “perhaps indefinitely.” 7 This conclusion is inaccurate and flawed. In fact, the accumulation of significant carrying costs is a direct consequence of prolonged delays in cash recovery. Such delays or regulatory lag actually create a financial incentive for the utility to pursue timely cost recovery to minimize future carrying costs that would otherwise accrue. 6 Docket No. 22-08-08, Final Decision, at 28. 7 Docket No. 22-08-08, Final Decision, at 28.

**Q.** Should PURA’s historical rate base treatment for deferred assets and liabilities continue to be applied?

**A.** Yes. Carrying costs on deferred expenses are an integral part of any regulatory asset or liability and must be included in the total cost of the deferral. Said differently, the deferred cost and the costs to finance that amount are inseparable. Thus, assuming the underlying costs are prudent, carrying charges must also be recognized for the Company to recover the costs of capital supplied to support such prudent expenses. Given PURA has not historically allowed the recognition of carrying charges from cost incurrence between rate cases on deferred assets the historical rate base treatment must continue to be applied as it provides a compensatory level of recovery of historical carrying charges for the regulatory lag associated with these deferrals. If PURA continues to remove deferred cost from rate base, then carrying charges from the date of cost incurrence should be allowed to compensate the utility for the costs of capital deployed to support all prudently incurred deferred costs. 1. O’Conner, in Exhibit CLP-EMPLOYEE-1, variable compensation is a critical component of the Company’s annual labor costs incurred allowing the Company to attract and retain key talent in order to continue to provide safe and reliable service to our customers. From an accounting perspective, the Company is required to follow both the generally accepted accounting principles (“GAAP”) and the FERC Uniform System of Accounts (“USoA”). Each of these accounting standards provides rules and regulations the Company must comply with to ensure its financial records are appropriately stated. Labor costs, including variable pay, are required under the accounting rules to be separated between capital and expense, based on the type of work performed. Said differently, labor costs incurred that are construction related meet the capitalization criteria and are capitalized as a part of the capital work order, while the remaining labor costs are expensed as an operations and maintenance expense (“O&M”). As a result, in accordance with accounting principles and guidelines, the Company appropriately capitalizes the portion of variable pay issued to non-executive employees using a capitalization ratio derived from the non-executive employee’s capitalized base pay.

**Q.** How does ESC manage its liability insurance costs?

**A.** All insurance programs and policies are evaluated annually with the aid of insurance brokers in order to secure the best available coverage at the best available rate. Higher deductible levels result in lower insurance premiums while also resulting in a higher retention of risk of loss. It is the balance between the two that ESC must manage on behalf of CL&P and the other operating companies that it serves. ESC utilizes a well-accepted process when procuring insurance programs. The broker compiles the market submission and works with various insurance markets to solicit quotes for insuring the ESC program. Approximately three to four months prior to the renewal date of the program, ESC’s Insurance team holds a strategy meeting with the broker to discuss the current coverage in place, opportunities for improvement in coverage and upcoming renewal requirements, and strategies for presenting risk mitigation requirements to the market to optimize the coverage ESC has in place for the utility subsidiaries it serves, including CL&P. ESC participates in various industry group discussions to stay abreast of insurance issues and trends, including working groups within the Edison Electric Institute. ESC’s Insurance team also maintains knowledge of key corporate initiatives that lower Eversource’s risk profile, helping to ensure the underwriting process goes smoothly. In addition to this knowledge, and to the industry trend information provided by the broker, ESC also utilizes independent sources, such as Edison Electric Institute and other insurance surveys to evaluate market trends. ESC utilizes an actuary each year to perform a study of the loss experienced from the self-insurance layer of general liability to determine what should be maintained in a balance sheet reserve to cover those claims within the self-insurance layer by operating company, including CL&P. On a combined basis, these processes assist in assuring that Eversource’s corporate liability costs, including CL&P’s, are as reasonable as possible.

## VI. LEAD LAG STUDY

_Testimony page 127_

**Q.** You mentioned earlier in your testimony that you prepared a lead lag study. Is that correct?

**A.** Yes. We prepared a lead lag study (the “Lead Lag Study”) to update and establish the net lag days to be used for certain operating expenses’ cash working capital requirements that will be included in base rates. The Lead Lag Study is summarized and included in the revenue requirement analysis as Schedule H-1.4.

**Q.** What is cash working capital?

**A.** Cash working capital (“CWC”) is the amount of cash needed by CL&P to fund its dayto-day operations in the time period between when expenses are incurred to provide service to customers, and when the associated cash payment is actually received from customers for those services. The time period is measured based on a lead lag study that provides the difference between revenue lag days and expense lead days to determine the CWC requirement. The CWC is a component of rate base.

**Q.** What is a lead lag study?

**A.** A lead lag study is an acceptable method for developing the CWC component of rate base in connection with the determination of revenue requirements in public utility rate case proceedings. The objective of a lead lag study is to measure, (1) the number of days between the point in time customers receive service from CL&P and the point in time customers’ cash payments are received by CL&P (“revenue lag days”), and (2) the number of days between the point in time CL&P receives goods or services and the point in time CL&P pays for those goods or services (“expense lead days”). The difference between the revenue lag days and the expense lead days results in a net lag/lead day by cost category, which the Company then multiplies by the average daily expense in the Rate Year for that category. The sum of these results equals the Company’s required CWC, which is included as a component of rate base.

**Q.** Please define the terms “revenue lag days” and “expense lead days.”

**A.** Revenue lag is the time, measured in days, between delivery of a service to CL&P customers and the receipt of cash from those customers for the payment of such service (cash inflows). Because CL&P’s customers receive services prior to CL&P’s receipt of cash for those services, CL&P experiences a revenue lag in its daily operating costs. The longer the revenue lag (i.e., the amount of time it takes for CL&P to receive cash payments from customers), the more cash the Company needs to fund its day-to-day operations. Similarly, expense lead is the time, again measured in days, between the performance of a service or receipt of goods, and the cash payments made by CL&P for such goods or services (cash outflows). The longer the expense lead, the less cash the Company needs to fund its day-to-day operations. Both revenues and expenses are assumed to be served evenly; thus, where monthly or annual data is presented, it is assumed that the revenue or expense was incurred at the midpoint of the month or year. Because base rates are based on revenue and expenses recognized on the accrual basis of accounting (book basis), the revenue lag results in a need for capital, while the expense lead offsets this need to the extent the Company is typically not required to reimburse its vendors until after a service is provided or a good is delivered (cash basis).

**Q.** Please describe the Lead Lag Study (Schedule H-1.4) and its findings.

**A.** The Lead Lag Study results are contained in Schedule H-1.4. The Company presents a total CWC amount for the Rate Year of $50,839,000 (Schedule H-1.4, page 2 of 2, line 38). The Lead Lag Study involves a great deal of effort and analysis of most major cost sources, including labor, general O&M expenses, and taxes. The process involves analyzing payroll data and reviewing invoices in order to determine the exact date or midpoint of the period during which the goods or services were provided. Each transaction analyzed is then traced to the actual cash disbursement date, when CL&P made the payment, to determine the number of days from receipt of the goods or services to the cash outflow related to them. Please refer to “A. Revenue Lag Days” and “B. Expense Lead Days” below for a detailed description of each major component of the Lead Lag Study.

### A. Revenue Lag Days

**Q.** How is the revenue lag computed?

**A.** The revenue lag results are contained in Schedule H-1.4a. The revenue lag consists of a “meter reading (or service) lag,” “billing lag” and “collection lag.” The sum of the days associated with these three lag components is the total revenue lag experienced by CL&P during the test year.

**Q.** What lag does the Lead Lag Study reveal for the component "meter reading (or service) lag?"

**A.** The first component of the revenue lag is the meter reading or service lag. When a service is provided to customers (or to CL&P as it relates to costs) over a steady period of time, the service is deemed to have been provided (or received) evenly over the midpoint of the service period, unless specific information regarding the provision (or receipt) of that service indicates otherwise. This is commonly referred to as the ‘midpoint method’ in a lead lag study. Applying the midpoint method means that the service received by CL&P customers has been equally and evenly provided over the entire monthly period – the halfway point between the beginning of the service period and the end of the service period. The midpoint of a service period can be calculated using the following formula, where the number of days in a year equals “A” and the number of periods in a year equals “B”: A/B Midpoint Method = 2 Under the midpoint method, the Lead Lag Study reveals 15.21 days (365 days / 12 months / 2) as the midpoint of CL&P’s service period (see Schedule H-1.4a, page 1 of 10, line 18).

**Q.** How did you arrive at the 1.51 day “billing lag”?

**A.** Most of the Company’s customers are billed the next business day after the meters are read. In addition, the Company’s meter reading schedule does not allow meter readings on weekends and holidays. The Company’s calendar year 2025 meter reading schedule was used to calculate the total billing lag, which was 1.51 days. In addition, we have not made an exception for large customers, which would require additional time to process and likely increase the 1.51 day billing lag. We have chosen to avoid this level of complexity with the understanding that our simplifying assumption of ignoring the large customer billing lag, which will understate CWC and will not disadvantage the Company’s customers. Therefore, the Company arrived at a final 1.51 day “billing lag” from the Lead Lag Study. See Schedule H-1.4a, page 1 of 13, line 20.

**Q.** How was the “collection lag” calculated and what was the result?

**A.** The “collection lag” for utility service totaled 26.66 days. See Schedule H-1.4a, page 1 of 13, line 16. This lag reflects the time delay between the mailing of customer bills and the cash received from customers. The 26.66 days lag was derived using the ‘accounts receivable turnover method,’ which involved an examination of utility service customer accounts receivable balances for all accounts. Schedule H-1.4a, page 2 of 13, details month-end customer accounts receivable balances as provided in general ledger extracts and calculates the annual average accounts receivable balance for the Test Year (lines 18 – 32). Because the customer accounts receivable balances are recorded to the Company’s general ledger as of a point in time (end of the month), the calculation uses the average balance over a 13-month period (instead of 12 months) to capture all activity during the Test Year (from the first day of the year until the last day of the year). In addition, this same page summarizes the month-end reserve balances for uncollectible accounts. Schedule H-1.4a, page 2 of 13, details month-end balances as provided in the general ledger extracts and calculates the annual average reserve balance for the Test Year (lines 37 – 50). The same concept applies to the customer accounts receivable reserve balances as described above, whereby the Company’s general ledger month-end balances were captured in the calculation using a 13-month period to capture all activity during the Test Year. The sum of these two data points was then used to determine the “Net Average Receivable Balance” (line 52). The final data point in the calculation is using the Company’s total billed revenue for the Test Year (there are no unbilled revenues) found on line 60, “Total Sales.” In determining the receivable turnover ratio, which is a measure to quantify how efficiently CL&P is in receiving cash payments from customers (i.e., the number of times that accounts receivables are converted to cash during the Test Year), the calculation takes the Total Sales and divides it by the Net Average Receivable Balance. The Receivables Turnover ratio totaled 13.69 times (line 62). In order to determine how long it takes to collect the cash from customers from the time the bill is generated and mailed to customers, the total number of days in the year is divided by the Receivables Turnover ratio (365 days/13.69 times = 26.66 days “collection lag”).

**Q.** Is the total revenue lag computed from these separate lag calculations?

This total number of lag days represents the time between when a customer receives service from CL&P and when CL&P receives cash from that customer for payment of that service (cash inflows).

This access is required for both debt and equity investors. By having access to low-cost debt and equity, the Company is able to maintain the safety and reliability of its system while providing that safe, reliable service at the lowest possible cost to customers. The costs associated with the Company’s investor-relations–related activities is a necessary cost and one that is appropriately paid for by customers.
